# Where an MRI Can Go: State Approval Rules for Advanced Imaging in Nine States

Before a hospital, clinic, or imaging company adds an MRI or CT scanner, some states require permission first, through a certificate-of-need (CON) review that asks whether the region needs the capacity. Others require only a license, a spending report, or radiation-safety registration, and some require nothing beyond what any medical practice already needs. This page compares nine states: Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, Maryland, and Georgia, plus Washington, Colorado, Minnesota, and Arizona. It covers whether state approval applies to MRI, CT, and mobile units, what dollar thresholds apply, what changed from 2023 to 2026, and what the rules mean for putting a scanner in a rural county. Current as of October 26, 2026.

## What stands out

- **Four ways of deciding where a scanner goes.** Only Virginia still runs a full need review for MRI and CT, with drive-time and procedure-volume standards. Georgia reviews imaging based on who owns the scanner: hospitals and physician practices are exempt at any cost, while independent imaging centers still need a certificate. Maryland, Minnesota, and Arizona skip need review but keep a license or reporting requirement. Pennsylvania, Ohio, Washington, and Colorado leave imaging to radiation-safety rules alone.
- **Mobile units are where the rules get tested.** A mobile MRI can serve several rural hospitals in a week, which makes it one of the more practical ways to bring advanced imaging to small communities. Virginia reviews the site rather than the scanner: each new host site needs its own certificate, with volume standards prorated by days on site. In Georgia, decisions on whether independent providers can add mobile or exempt MRI units are before the state's appellate courts, and the Supreme Court heard argument in four of those cases on October 15, 2026. In most of the other states, a mobile unit is treated the same as a fixed one.
- **Rural access is entering the debate through other doors.** No state on this list has a rural exemption written specifically for imaging. Virginia has gone furthest in a different direction: a 2025 law asked a state task force to recommend faster review in underserved areas, and 2026 laws created expedited review for projects in "medical deserts," with regulations due January 1, 2027. Georgia's 2024 reform eased rules for rural hospitals but kept imaging centers in rural counties under review. The rural question is being decided through hospital and access policy rather than imaging policy.
- **Reporting is the quieter alternative.** Minnesota tracks imaging capacity without approving it: providers report purchases over $1 million, and non-hospital imaging sites, including mobile operators, report their use every year. Maryland requires an equipment license tied to a state finding. These approaches let a state see where capacity is growing without deciding in advance whether it should.

## What to watch

- Late 2026 to 2027: Georgia Supreme Court. A decision in the Amera Imaging cases, argued October 15, 2026, along with pending Court of Appeals cases, could clarify how far the 2024 hospital and physician exemptions reach for independent imaging providers.
- January 1, 2027: Virginia. Board of Health regulations for expedited "medical desert" review are due, following State Health Services Plan Task Force recommendations due October 1, 2026. Whether imaging projects qualify will shape what the new process means for rural MRI.
- 2027 session: Minnesota. A 2026 bill to repeal the hospital construction moratorium could be reintroduced. It covers hospital beds, not imaging.
- 2027 session: Washington. Lawmakers may act on the Department of Health's 2025 modernization recommendations, which focus on emergency and urgent care, not imaging.
- 2027 session: Maryland. The 2026 bills on health care transactions and psychiatric services died in committee and could return; none touch imaging.
- Fall 2026: Pennsylvania Senate. HB 1460, which would add review of for-profit health facility acquisitions, awaits a Senate floor vote. It is not a CON bill and does not cover imaging equipment.

The open question is whether states keep using need review to manage where scanners go, or shift toward licensing and reporting that let capacity follow demand while the state keeps watch.
